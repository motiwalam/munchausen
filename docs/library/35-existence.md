# 35. One heuristic and existence

This chapter answers questions 91, 92, 108, 101 and 100 of `questions.md`
(workload 4 of `questions-round6.md`). Questions 91 and 92 ask whether the
root heuristic of short strings (sections 11.4, 19.3) and the per-base
model of long strings (chapter 5) are one heuristic. Question 108 asks for
one invariant-residue statement for every positional system. Question 101
asks whether averaging over bases pushes Theorem 20.3 past its barrier.
Question 100 asks for near-solutions that provably exist in every base.

Each statement carries one label: Theorem, Proposition, Lemma or Corollary
(proved here), Heuristic, Conjecture, or computed (an exact computation or
a measurement, with its range). Scripts are `analysis/r6h_*.py`, data
`analysis/data/r6h_*`. Every run used one or two threads.

Notation. `w(x) = x^x` for `x >= 1` and `w(0) = m`, the convention
`0^0 = m`. For a digit multiset `c`, `F(c)` is the sum of the weights,
`sigma(c)` the sum of the digits, `g(x) = w(x) - x` and
`G(c) = F(c) - sigma(c)`, the sum of `g` over `c`. The library writes
`S(c)` for `F(c)` in chapter 5 and question 91, and for `sigma(c)` in
chapter 20 and question 101. This chapter avoids the letter. An arrangement
of `c` is a string with the multiset `c` and a nonzero leading digit, and
`P_s(r)` is the value of the string `s` read in base `r`. `e(t)` is
`exp(2 pi i t)`. `X_k(b)` is the largest digit `x` with `x^x < b^k`. As in
chapter 20, "solution" means a nontrivial solution with at least 2 digits.

## 35.1 Arrangements and the local factor (question 91)

### 35.1.1 The theorem

Fix a base `b`, a modulus `q` and a multiset `c` of `k >= 3` digits. Draw an
arrangement of `c` uniformly. Section 11.4 and direction A2 call
`q P(n == F(c) (mod q))` the local factor of `c` at `q`. For `q | b - 1`
every arrangement has `n == sigma(c)`, so the factor is `q` or 0, the
identity of section 1.6. For `q | b` the residue `n mod q` is the last digit
modulo `q`. The question is the case `gcd(q, b (b - 1)) = 1`. Since
`b (b - 1)` is even, such a `q` is odd. So the moduli 4 and 8 of question 91
never qualify.

Condition on the leading digit `x0`. The other `k' = k - 1` digits form a
multiset `c'` and fill the positions `0, ..., k - 2` uniformly. Put

    gamma = max over h != 0 (mod q) of | (1/k') sum over x of c'_x e(h x / q) |.

`gamma = 1` exactly when all digits of `c'` are congruent modulo some prime
factor of `q`. For digits spread over the residues `gamma` is small.

Theorem 35.1 (proved). Let `gcd(q, b (b - 1)) = 1`, and let `m >= 0` with
`2m <= k'`. For every residue `a`,

    | q P(n == a (mod q) | leading digit x0) - 1 |
        <= (q - 1) prod over j < m of sqrt( (1 + rho_j^2) / 2 ),
    rho_j = min(1, (gamma k' + 2j) / (k' - 2j)).

Proof. Let `s` be a uniform arrangement of `c'` on the positions
`0..k'-1`. Pair the positions `(2j, 2j+1)` for `j < m`, and let
`tau_0, ..., tau_(m-1)` be independent fair coins, independent of `s`. Let
`s'` be `s` with the digits of pair `j` exchanged when `tau_j = 1`. The
arrangements of `c'` form one orbit of the permutations of the positions,
and composing a uniform element of an orbit with an independent
permutation keeps it uniform. So `s'` is uniform too. Exchanging the
digits `u = s_(2j)` and `v = s_(2j+1)` changes the value by

    delta_j = (v - u)(b^(2j) - b^(2j+1)) = (v - u) b^(2j) (1 - b).

Given `s`,

    E[ e(h n(s') / q) | s ] = e(h n(s) / q) prod over j < m of (1 + e(h delta_j / q)) / 2,

and `|(1 + e(t))/2| = |cos(pi t)|`. Now reveal the pairs in order. Given the
first `j` pairs, pair `j` is an ordered pair of two distinct items drawn
from the remaining `k'' = k' - 2j >= 2` digits, with counts `a_x`. Put
`H = h b^(2j) (1 - b)`. It is nonzero modulo `q`, because `h` is and `b`
and `1 - b` are units modulo `q`. Then

    E[ cos(2 pi h delta_j / q) | past ] = (|A(H)|^2 - k'') / (k'' (k'' - 1)) <= |A(H)|^2 / k''^2,

with `A(H) = sum over x of a_x e(H x / q)`. The equality sums `e(H (v - u)/q)`
over the ordered pairs of distinct items. The inequality is `|A| <= k''`.
Removing `2j` digits changes `A` by at most `2j`, so
`|A(H)| <= gamma k' + 2j`. Since `cos^2(pi t) = (1 + cos(2 pi t))/2`, the
conditional mean of `cos^2(pi h delta_j / q)` is at most `(1 + rho_j^2)/2`.
By Cauchy-Schwarz the conditional mean of `|cos(pi h delta_j / q)|` is at
most `sqrt((1 + rho_j^2)/2)`. The factors lie in `[0, 1]`, so conditioning
pair by pair bounds the mean of the product by the product of these bounds.
Finally `P(n == a) = (1/q) sum over h of e(-h a/q) E e(h n/q)`, and the term
`h = 0` is `1/q`. The leading digit adds a fixed phase.

The draft of this chapter wrote `H = 2 h b^(2j)(1 - b)` and used that `q`
is odd. The factor 2 was an error. It did not affect the bound, since `2h`
is also nonzero modulo the odd `q`.

Corollary 35.2 (proved). For every residue `a`,

    | q P(n == a (mod q)) - 1 | <= (q - 1) e^(1/8) exp( -(1 - gamma)^2 (k - 1) / 64 ).

This holds also without conditioning on the leading digit, with `gamma` the
largest over the leading digits `x0`: the unconditional law is a mixture of
the conditional ones.

Proof. Take `eps = (1 - gamma)/4` and `m = floor(eps k' / 2)`, so that
`2m <= k'`. For `j < m`,
`rho_j <= (gamma + eps)/(1 - eps) = (3 gamma + 1)/(3 + gamma) <= (1 + gamma)/2`.
The last step is `(1 - gamma)^2 >= 0`. So
`(1 + rho_j^2)/2 <= 1 - (1 - gamma)/4`, and each factor is at most
`exp(-(1 - gamma)/8)`. Then `m >= (1 - gamma) k'/8 - 1`.

Check (computed, `r6h_bound_check.py`, 400 random multisets with `k = 4..8`
in bases 20 to 30, `q` from 3 to 13 prime to `b (b - 1)`, all arrangements
enumerated). Neither bound is ever exceeded. Without conditioning, the
deviation `max over a of |q P(n == a) - 1|` is at most 0.64 times the bound
of Theorem 35.1 with the best `m`. With conditioning, the bound is attained
when all other digits are congruent modulo `q`, where both sides are
`q - 1`.

Remarks.

- The rate does not involve `ord_q(b)`. Question 91 expected an error
  exponentially small in `k / ord_q(b)`, from the class sums of positions
  with equal `b^i mod q`. The exchange of two adjacent digits avoids the
  classes: it always moves `n` by a unit times the digit difference. The
  only place where `b` enters is the requirement that `b` and `b - 1` be
  units modulo `q`. The theorem and its proof use nothing else about `b`,
  so they hold for `P_s(r)` at every residue `r` with `r` and `r - 1` prime
  to `q`.
- Long strings (Heuristic). For a string with `k` near `b` the digits are
  close to uniform on `0..b-1`. Since `|sum over x < b of e(h x/q)| <= q/2`,
  `gamma` is at most `q/(2b)` plus a fluctuation of order `k^(-1/2)`. So for
  `q <= b/10` prime to `b (b - 1)`, `gamma <= 0.1` once `k` is large, and
  every residue has `q P(n == a) = 1 + O(q e^(-k/80))`. This is the sense in
  which "arrangements make the local factors vanish for long strings".
- The constants are not optimized. When the digits are small compared with
  `q`, `gamma` is close to 1 although the arrangements still spread: the
  units `b^(2j)(1 - b)` vary with `j`, which the bound ignores.

### 35.1.2 The root factor is the base average of the per-base factor

For a string `s` let `N_s(q)` be the number of `r (mod q)` with
`P_s(r) == F(s) (mod q)`. For a prime `q` this is the factor `rho_s(q)` of
section 11.4, and for a prime power the p-adic factor of section 19.3. For a
residue `r` let `share_r(a)` be the share of the arrangements of `c` with
`P_s(r) == a (mod q)`. It depends only on `r mod q`.

Proposition 35.3 (proved). For every multiset `c` and every `q >= 2`,

    mean over the arrangements s of c of N_s(q) = sum over r mod q of share_r(F(c) mod q).

The term `r == 1` is `1[q | G(c)]`. For a prime `q` the term `r == 0` is the
share of the arrangements whose last digit is `== F(c) (mod q)`, and the
other `q - 2` terms are `1/q` each, up to the error of Theorem 35.1.

Proof. Exchange the sum over `r` and the mean over the arrangements. For
`r == 1`, `P_s(1) = sigma(c)` for every arrangement. For `r == 0`,
`P_s(0)` is the last digit. For a prime `q` every other `r` has `r` and
`r - 1` prime to `q`, and the first remark after Corollary 35.2 applies.

So the root heuristic, at a fixed string, averages over the residue of the
base. The per-base refined model fixes the residue. At bases
`b == 1 (mod q)` its factor is `q 1[q | G(c)]`; at other bases it is the
arrangement factor of Theorem 35.1 or the last-digit factor. Averaged over
`b mod q`, the per-base factors give `(1/q)(q 1[q|G] + q share_0 + ...)`,
which is the mean of `N_s(q)`. For a random multiset the mean is about
`1 + (P(q | G(c)) - 1/q)`, up to the last-digit and arrangement errors. So
the root heuristic's excess at the prime `q` is the excess of the identity
modulo `q` at the bases where `q | b - 1`.

Computed (`r6h_arrange.py rho`, `data/r6h_arrange_rho.txt`, 30 random
candidate multisets per cell, bases 9 to 30). The identity holds to
`4 * 10^-16` against a brute-force mean over all arrangements for
`k <= 6`. The part of the residues `r` other than `r = 1` with `r` and
`r - 1` prime to `q` is 0.32 to 0.41 for `q = 3` (1/3 expected), 0.59 to
0.69 for `q = 5` (3/5), and 0.60 to 0.75 for `q = 7` (5/7) at `k = 3..6`. It
is within 0.01 of these values for `k = 7..12`.

### 35.1.4 Where the factor 2 to 4 comes from

Direction A2 and question 91 explain the excess of short solutions over the
per-base model by arrangement factors that stay away from 1 at short
lengths. Proposition 35.3 and Table 35.1.3.1 say otherwise: those factors
have mean 1, so they add scatter, not an excess. Part of the excess has a
different source. At a short length the factor of the identity modulo
`b - 1`, `(b - 1) P(b - 1 | G(c))`, is far above its long-string limit. A
sum of few values `g(x) = x^x - x` of small digits is concentrated modulo
the small primes of `b - 1`. For example `x^x == x (mod 3)` for 5 of every
6 digits `x >= 1` (it fails only for `x == 2 (mod 6)`). The basic model
leaves this out. The refined model of section 5.3 contains it exactly if
it is computed at the short length itself.

the short exceptions over the basic model, and the structured strings
explain most of the rest under `0^0 = 0`. Under `0^0 = 1` a generic excess
of about 3 times remains (4 against 1.35). The arrangement factors cannot
explain it, since their mean is 1. It stays an unexplained fluctuation, the
same as the 5-digit excess of section 22.4.3.

Heuristic 35.4. The root heuristic and the per-base refined model are two
averages of one local density. The root heuristic averages the per-base
factors over the residue of the base modulo each `q`; the per-base model
keeps the residue of the actual base. They agree on totals over complete
residue systems of bases. Per base they differ by the arrangement factors
at `q` prime to `b (b - 1)`, which have mean 1 and an RMS of about
`e^(-0.35 k)`, and by the last-digit factor at `q | b`. The excess of short
solutions over the basic per-base model is not an arrangement effect. It
has three parts: the identity factor modulo `b - 1` at the short length (a
factor 1.1 to 2.1 for `x^x`; for the narcissistic weights of section 19.4
the refined model gives 671 against 760 observed, where the basic model
gives 253), identity families and self-paying blocks, and under `0^0 = 1` a
residual of about 3 times on 4 generic strings (P = 0.048). The crossover
where the arrangement factors become negligible is about 10 digits. The
identity factor converges more slowly in the small primes of `b - 1`. Under
`0^0 = 1` its long-string limit, averaged over the parities, is about 1.07
(1 in even bases, `1 + e^-2 = 1.135` in odd ones, Theorem 34.12). The data
of Table 35.1.4.1 have ratios 1.05 to 1.11 at every length from 8 to 12,
against 1.4 to 1.5 at 3 and 4 digits.

## 35.2 The two heuristics at medium lengths (question 92)

### 35.2.1 The computation

`r6h_medium.py` computes, for every base `b` from 7 to 20, every length `k`
from 6 to 12 with `k <= b` (83 cells per convention) and both conventions:

- root: the root heuristic of sections 11.4 and 19.3 for the strings with
  digits below `b` whose real root lies in `[b - 1/2, b + 1/2)`, by Monte
  Carlo (1.5 million strings per cell under `0^0 = 1`, 0.6 million under
  `0^0 = 0`; digits up to `min(b - 1, X)` with `X^X < (b + 1/2)^k`). Three
  versions: spacing only, local factors modulo the primes up to 31
  ("primes"), and roots modulo 64, 81, 125 in place of 2, 3, 5 ("p-adic").
- ref: the refined per-base model, exact (`r6q_lib.refined_by_zeros`), and
  the same times the forced-top factor for the top `t = 1, 2, 3` digits
  (the factor of `forced_top.py`, sampled over the candidate multisets that
  meet the identity, one million draws per cell).
- win: the window calibration of section 22.4.1 (`r4b_spectrum.c`, class
  mean modulo `2(b - 1)`, window `b <= |D| <= 0.05 b^(k-1)`), lengths 6 to 10
  only. Length 11 would take about 40 minutes and length 12 several hours.
- obs: the census of `output/b2-41-<m>.txt`.

0.99, 0.83 and 0.31 under `0^0 = 0`. The sum of the absolute differences is
smallest for the refined model with `t = 1` in both conventions (0.92 and

are not.

### 35.2.3 What the numbers say

- The two heuristics predict the same total at lengths 6 to 12, within 10%
  under `0^0 = 1` (8.85 against 9.51 and 9.74) and within 12% under
  `0^0 = 0` (12.72 against 13.00 and 14.19). The census, 10 and 12, fits
  both (computed against heuristic). So direction A2 holds at medium
  lengths in the sense of Heuristic 35.4: on totals over many bases the
  root heuristic with local factors and the refined per-base model are the
  same prediction.
- They split the total differently between bases, as Proposition 35.3
  predicts. The refined model gives the parity doubling to the odd bases
  only; the root heuristic averages its 2-adic factor over both parities.
  Under `0^0 = 0`, lengths 6 to 10, the window calibration has 2.38 in even
  bases and 5.77 in odd ones (ratio 2.4). The refined model with `t = 1`
  has 2.55 and 5.56. The root heuristic with primes has 4.37 and 5.27, and
  the p-adic version 4.33 and 6.79. The census has 2 and 8. So per base the
  refined model matches the window calibration and the root heuristic does
  not.
- The forced top digits matter at these lengths in single cells. In base 10
  at 6 digits under `0^0 = 1` the refined model gives 0.12, the forced-top
  model 0.000 to 0.007, the window calibration 0.002, and the root
  heuristic 0.05 to 0.15. The candidate digits are at most 7, and a single
  7 makes `F >= 7^7 = 823543`, so the top digit of `F` is 8 or 9, which no
  candidate can contain. The root heuristic smooths over the base and
  cannot see this. On totals the forced-top factors change little (0 to

- So the answer to question 92 is: the refined model with the top digit
  forced matches the window calibration best, cell by cell and by parity.
  The root heuristic matches it on totals only. No missing factor shows at
  these lengths. The 5-digit excess of section 22.4.3 is therefore not a
  factor that the medium lengths share (Heuristic).

## 35.3 One invariant-residue proposition (question 108)

### 35.3.1 The proposition

A positional system here is: a commutative ring `R` (the integers,
`Z[i]`, `Z[omega]`, `Z[eps]`, `Z[1/q]` or `F_q[T]`), place values
`beta_i` in `R` for the positions `i` of an index set, a digit set `D` in
`R`, and a set `A` of admissible strings. A string `s` has a finite set of
positions, a digit `s_i` at each, the value `v(s) = sum s_i beta_i`, and the
multiset of its digits. Two admissible strings are equivalent when they
have the same multiset. An elementary exchange is a pair of admissible
strings on the same positions that differ by swapping the digits at two
positions `i, j`. For an ideal `I` of `R`, say that the multiset fixes `v`
modulo `I` when equivalent admissible strings have congruent values.

Proposition 35.5 (proved). Let `I_0` be the ideal generated by all
`v(s) - v(s')` with `s, s'` equivalent and admissible, and `J` the ideal
generated by `(s_i - s_j)(beta_j - beta_i)` over the elementary exchanges.

1. The multiset fixes `v` modulo `I` exactly when `I` contains `I_0`.
2. `J` lies in `I_0`, and `J = I_0` when the admissible strings of each
   multiset are connected by elementary exchanges.
3. Let `beta_i = u beta^i` with `u` a unit, `i` in an interval of integers
   (negative `i` only when `beta` is a unit). Then every value satisfies
   `v(s) == u sum s_i (mod beta - 1)`, so `I_0` lies in `(beta - 1)`. If some
   admissible string allows the exchange of two adjacent positions `i`,
   `i + 1` whose digits differ by a unit, and `beta^i` is a unit (always for
   `i = 0`), then `I_0 = (beta - 1)`.

Proof. (1) and the first half of (2) are the definitions. An elementary
exchange changes the value by
`s_i beta_j + s_j beta_i - s_i beta_i - s_j beta_j`. If the exchange graph
of each class is connected, every difference `v(s) - v(s')` is a sum of
elementary differences along a path. (3) For `i >= 0`,
`beta^i - 1` is a multiple of `beta - 1`, and for a unit `beta` so is
`beta^(-1) - 1 = -beta^(-1)(beta - 1)`. So `beta_i == u (mod beta - 1)`. An
exchange at positions `i, i + 1` with digit difference a unit `e` puts
`e u beta^i (beta - 1)` into `J`, a unit multiple of `beta - 1`.

In the integer, negative, bijective and balanced bases the exchange of
the last two digits of a string with at least 3 digits is admissible, and
some string has two last digits that differ by 1. So (3) gives `I_0`
exactly. When the admissible strings are the expansions of rational
integers in a system whose place values are not integers (`p/q` and `-p/q`
with `q > 1`, the Gaussian, Eisenstein and Pisot bases), an exchange
usually leaves `Z`. Then (2) gives no lower bound for `I_0`, and Table
35.3.2.1 checks `I_0` meet `Z` directly.

Question 108 states (1) with "exactly when `I` contains every difference
`beta_i - beta_j` of two positions that can exchange". That needs the
digit differences and the connectivity of (2). Without a unit digit
difference the ideal can be smaller (digits all even give `2 (beta - 1)`),
and without connectivity it can be larger than `J`.

Proposition 35.6 (proved). Let `beta` be an algebraic integer with monic
minimal polynomial `f` over `Z`, and `R = Z[beta]`. Then
`(beta - 1) R` meets `Z` in `|f(1)| Z`. For `R = Z[1/q]` and
`beta = p/q` or `-p/q` with `gcd(p, q) = 1`, `(beta - 1)` meets `Z` in
`(p - q) Z` or `(p + q) Z`.

Proof. `Z[t]/(f(t), t - 1) = Z/f(1)`. In `Z[1/q]` the element
`p/q - 1 = (p - q)/q` is a unit times `p - q`, and
`Z[1/q]/(p - q) = Z/(p - q)` since `gcd(q, p - q) = 1`. The same holds for
`-p/q - 1 = -(p + q)/q`.

So the integer modulus of a geometric system is `|f(1)|`: `b - 1` for
`beta = b`, `b + 1` for `-b`, `(n+1)^2 + 1` for `-n + i`, `n^2 + 3n + 3` for
`-n + omega`, `x` for `eps_x` (`f = t^2 - x t - 1`), `B - 1` in `F_q[T]`,
`p - q` for `p/q` (with the place values `(1/q)(p/q)^i` of section 21.2,
`u = 1/q`), and `p + q` for `-p/q`. The identity modulus, the swap-lemma
class of offsets (Lemmas 26.1 and 26.4: equivalent strings have offsets
`D` congruent modulo `I_0`, and the elementary exchanges realize the
generators) and the residue condition of the refined model are all this
ideal.

Proposition 35.7 (proved, the residue factor). Let the system satisfy
`v(s) == u sum s_i (mod I_0)` as in Proposition 35.5 (3), and put
`g(x) = w(x) - u x`. Let the digits of a string be independent with a
fixed law that gives positive weight to a digit `x1` with `g(x1) = 0` (the
digit 1 when `u = 1`). Let `H` be the subgroup of `R/I_0` generated by the
`g(x)` of the digits with positive weight, and suppose `R/I_0` is finite.
Then `P(sum g(x_i) == 0 (mod I_0)) -> 1/|H|` as the length grows, so the
refined model gives the factor `[R/I_0 : H]`.

Proof. The partial sums are a random walk on the finite group `H` whose
step law charges 0 and generates `H`. The walk is irreducible and
aperiodic, so it converges to the uniform law on `H`.

This is the parity doubling (index 2 when all `g(x)` are even), the
factor `q^k(B)` of `F_q[T]` (Theorem 21.6: the trace conditions cut out
`H`), and the absence of any factor in the factorial system (`I_0 = R`).
Proposition 30.6 is the case of the weights `x^d`. For `x^x` in base `b`
the law of the digits changes with `b`, so Proposition 35.7 is the model
statement, and Theorem 30.5 is the theorem.

## 35.4 Beyond `ln b / ln ln b` by averaging over bases (question 101)

### 35.4.1 The averaged count

Notation of section 20.1: `L = ln B`, `K = kappa L / ln L`, `X = X_K(B)`,
`m(c) = F(c) - sigma(c) = G(c)`. For a base `b` choose a threshold `z(b)`
with `k z^z < b - 1`, and call the digits up to `z` small. For a multiset
`c` write `c = c_L + c_S` (large and small parts). Then
`F(c_S) <= k z^z < b - 1`, since `w(0) <= 1`.

Proposition 35.8 (proved). For `k >= 3` and `b` large, the base of a
`k`-digit solution with multiset `c` and leading digit `p` lies in the
window of Lemma 20.1 computed from `F(c_L)`, widened by one base on each
side. Given `c_L` and `b`, the congruence `b - 1 | m(c)` fixes `m(c_S)`,
because `m(c_S)` lies in `[0, b - 2]`. So the number of bases `b` in
`(sqrt B, B]` with a `k`-digit solution is at most

    sum over c_L, p, and b in the widened window of r(b, c_L),
    r(b, c_L) = #{ c_S : m(c_S) == -m(c_L) (mod b - 1) } <= max over N of r_j(N),

with `j = k - |c_L|` and `r_j(N)` the number of small multisets of `j`
digits with `m(c_S) = N`. By Lemma 30.2 the digits of `c_S` from
`z0 = max(2, ceil((j - 1)/2))` up are fixed by `m(c_S)` once the digits
below `z0` are fixed, so `r_j(N) <= C(z0 + j, j)`.

Proof. `F(c) - F(c_L) = F(c_S) < b`. By the mean value theorem this moves
`(F/p)^(1/(k-1))` by at most `b / ((k - 1) p (F(c_L)/p)^((k-2)/(k-1)))`,
which is below 1 for `k >= 3` and large `b`, since
`F(c_L) >= p b^(k-1) - b`. `g(x) >= 0` for every digit, so
`0 <= m(c_S) <= F(c_S) <= b - 2`. The bound on `r_j` is Lemma 30.2 with
`n <= j <= 2 z0 + 1`: the number of multisets of at most `j` digits below
`z0` is `C(z0 + j, j)`.

### 35.4.2 Why the gain is bounded

Proposition 35.9 (proved). Let `A > 0`, `z = z_A(B)` the largest `z` with
`z^z <= B^A`, and `K = kappa L / ln L` with `kappa` bounded. Among the
multisets of `K` digits from `0..X`, the share with no digit `<= z` lies
between `exp(-K (z + 1)/(X - z))` and `exp(-K (z + 1)/(X + K))`, and both
exponents tend to `-2A`. So the share is `e^(-2A + o(1))`.

Proof. The share is `C(X - z - 1 + K, K) / C(X + K, K)`, the product over
`i = 1..K` of `(X - z - 1 + i)/(X + i) = 1 - (z + 1)/(X + i)`. Each factor
lies between `1 - (z+1)/(X+1)` and `1 - (z+1)/(X+K)`. Use
`exp(-t/(1 - t)) <= 1 - t <= exp(-t)`. From `X ln X = K L (1 + o(1))` we
get `K / X = (ln X / L)(1 + o(1))` and `ln X = (2 + o(1)) ln L`, so
`K / X -> 0`. From `z ln z = A L (1 + o(1))` we get
`z = A L / ln z (1 + o(1))` and `ln z = (1 + o(1)) ln L`. Hence
`K z / X = A ln X / ln z (1 + o(1)) -> 2A`, and the same limit holds with
`X - z` or `X + K` in place of `X`.

So every multiset that matters to Theorem 20.3 has about `2A` digits of
weight below `B^A` on average, and a share `e^(-2A)` has none. For those,
the averaged count of Proposition 35.8 is the count of Theorem 20.3 itself:
`c_S` is empty and `r = 1[b - 1 | m(c_L)]`, bounded by 1.

Corollary 35.10 (proved). Let the averaged bound of Proposition 35.8 use a
fixed threshold `z` for all `b` in `(sqrt B, B]` and the window factor of
Proposition 20.2, as in the table below, and define `A` by `z^z = B^A`.
Then for `K = kappa L / ln L` it is at least `e^(-2A - o(1))` times the
bound of Proposition 20.2 summed over `2 <= k <= K`. Small weights must stay
below `b <= B`, so `A < 1` and the route of question 101 gains at most a
factor `e^(2 + o(1))` over Theorem 20.3.

Proof. The multisets of `K` digits with no small digit contribute to the
averaged bound with `r <= 1` and the window factor of Proposition 20.2.
By Proposition 35.9 they are a share `e^(-2A - o(1))` of the term `k = K`
of Proposition 20.2. That term carries all but a share `o(1)` of the sum
over `k <= K`. The binomial `C(X_k + k, k)` grows with `k` by a factor at
least `X_k / (k + 1)`, which is at least `L / (2 ln X_K)` up to a constant,
and tends to infinity, while the window factor changes by a bounded factor.

A bounded factor moves the threshold of Theorem 20.3 (d) by `O(1/ln L)`
digits. So averaging over the window, with the small part of
`F(c) - sigma(c)` as the only gain, cannot go past `alpha = 1`.

### 35.4.3 What averaging over the window does give

The window does not see the small digits at all, and that gives a
statement beyond `ln b / ln ln b` for a class of strings.

Theorem 35.12 (proved). Fix `C >= 1` and `eps > 0`. Call a digit `x` of a
solution in base `b` large when `x^x >= sqrt(b)`. The number of bases
`b <= B` with a solution of length at most `(ln B)^C` that has at most
`(1/C - eps) ln B / ln ln B` large digits is at most
`B^(max(1/2, 1 - C eps) + o(1))`. So almost all bases `b` have no solution
of length at most `(ln b)^C` with at most `(1/C - eps) ln b / ln ln b`
digits of weight at least `sqrt(b)`, whatever the number of smaller digits.

Proof. Bases up to `sqrt(B)` are at most `sqrt(B)`. Take `b > sqrt(B)` and
a solution `n` of `k <= K2 = L^C` digits, multiset `c = c_L + c_S` with
`c_L` the large digits, `k' = |c_L| <= K' = (1/C - eps) L / ln L`, and
leading digit `p`. Every digit is at most `X = X_(K2)(B)`, because
`x^x <= n < b^k <= B^(K2)`. Since `X ln X < K2 L`, `ln X <= (C + 1) ln L`.
A digit string is a solution in at most one base (Proposition 1.3), so at
most `(X + 1)^2` bases have a 2-digit solution; take `k >= 3`. Every small
weight is below `sqrt(b)` (also `w(0) <= 1`), so
`F(c_L) >= F(c) - k sqrt(b) >= p b^(k-1) / 2`. Since
`F(c_S) <= k sqrt(b)`, the mean value theorem for
`t -> (t/p)^(1/(k-1))` moves `Y = (F(c)/p)^(1/(k-1))` by at most
`k sqrt(b) / ((k - 1) p (b/2)^(k-2)) < 1` from `Y' = (F(c_L)/p)^(1/(k-1))`
for large `B`. By Lemma 20.1, `b` lies in an interval of length
`(M + 1)/(p (k - 1)) + 2` determined by `(c_L, k, p)`, with `M <= X`. Map
the base to `(c_L, k, p, b)`; the map is injective. The number of images is
at most

    sum over k' <= K', k <= K2, p <= X of C(X + k', k') ((X + 1)/(p (k - 1)) + 3)
        <= (K' + 1) K2 (X + 1) (H_X + 3) C(X + K', K').

Now `ln C(X + K', K') <= K' ln(e (X + K')/K') = K' (C ln L + O(ln ln L))
= (1 - C eps) L (1 + o(1))`, and the other factors are `exp(O(ln L))`. The
statement for almost all `b` follows as in Theorem 20.3 (c), since the
conditions are monotone in `b`.

For `C = 1` this covers lengths up to `ln b`, beyond the
`ln b / ln ln b` of Theorem 20.3, for strings with at most
`(1 - eps) ln b / ln ln b` large digits. Typical strings of such lengths
have almost all digits large, so Theorem 35.12 says nothing about them. It
covers the structured strings: a few large digits padded with zeros, ones
and other small digits, which is the shape of the self-paying families of
chapter 2 and the binomial families of chapter 22. Those families live in
a set of bases of density 0 (perfect powers and the like), which agrees
with Theorem 35.12.

## 35.5 Near-solutions in every base (question 100)

### 35.5.1 The theorem

Write `d = b - 1` for the length, `P_j = b^(b-1-j)` for the place value of
the `j`-th digit from the top (digit 1 at position `b - 2`), and
`theta_i = (1 - i/b)^(b-i)`, so that `(b - i)^(b-i) = theta_i b^(b-i)`.
From `-x/(1-x) <= ln(1 - x) <= -x`, `e^(-i) <= theta_i <= e^(-i + i^2/b)`.

Theorem 35.13 (proved). Let `b >= 3` and `T >= 1` with

- (a) `T + 5 <= b (1 - e^(-1 + 1/b))` and `2 (T + 4) <= b`;
- (b) `1 + T (e (T + 4) b / (b - T - 5) + 1) + e b / (b - 2) + 1 + T <= b - 2`.

Then for each convention there is an `n` with `b - 1` digits and

    0 < | n - F(n) | < b^(b - 1 - T),

and `n >= (b/e - 1) b^(b-2)`, so `|n - F(n)| < n b^(1-T) / (b/e - 1)`.

Proof. Let `x*` be the largest `x` with `T x^x + b < P_(T+1)`. For
`x = b - T - 4` we have `x >= T + 4` by (a), so `b^x >= b` and
`T x^x + b < (T + 1) b^x < b^(b-T-3) < P_(T+1)`. Hence `x* >= b - T - 4`.
Also `(b - 1)^(b-1) >= b^(b-1)/e > P_(T+1)`, so `x* <= b - 2`. The zones
are `Z_j = [x*, b - 1]` for `1 <= j <= T` and `Z_(T+1) = {b - 1}`, with
`z_j = |Z_j| <= T + 4`, so `z_j <= b/2`.

Step 1 (start). `L` holds one copy of `b - 1`. Then
`F(L) = theta_1 b^(b-1)` has `b - 1` digits, and its digit 1,
`floor(b theta_1) <= b e^(-1 + 1/b)`, is at most `x* - 1` by (a).

Step 2 (steering). For `j = 2, ..., T + 1` in turn: if digit `j` of
`F(L)` lies in `Z_j`, choose the steering digit `x_j`, the largest digit
`x` with `x^x <= (b - z_j - 1) P_j`, and add copies of it to `L` until digit
`j` leaves `Z_j`. Let `s = x_j^(x_j) / P_j`. Since
`(x+1)^(x+1) <= e (x+1) x^x`, either `x_j = b - 1` and
`s >= b theta_1 >= b/e`, or `s > (b - z_j - 1)/(e b) =: delta_j`. Also
`s <= b - z_j - 1`. Let `N_j = floor(F / P_j)`, the number formed by digits
1 to `j`. One copy raises `N_j` by `floor(s)` or `floor(s) + 1`. Digit `j`
starts at some `r >= b - z_j`.

- If `s >= z_j`, one copy raises `N_j` by `J` with `z_j <= J <= b - z_j`, so
  digit `j` moves from `r` to `r + J - b <= b - z_j - 1`, out of `Z_j`.
- If `1 <= s < z_j`, each copy raises `N_j` by 1 to `z_j`. After at most
  `z_j` copies digit `j` has wrapped past `b - 1` to a value at most
  `z_j - 1 < b - z_j`.
- If `s < 1`, each copy raises `N_j` by 0 or 1, so `N_j` passes through
  every integer, and at most `z_j / delta_j + 1` copies bring digit `j` to 0.

In every case we stop at the first copy after which digit `j` is out of
`Z_j`. Before that copy digit `j` has not wrapped, and the last copy wraps
it once, so `N_(j-1)` grows by at most 1 during step `j`. Digit `j - 1` was at most `x* - 1` after its own
step (for `j = 2`, after step 1), so it becomes at most `x* <= b - 2` and
causes no further carry. Later steps change `N_j` by at most 1 for the same
reason. So after step 2, digits 1 to `T` are at most `x*`, digit `T + 1` is
at most `b - 2`, and no carry ever reached beyond digit 1, so `F(L)` keeps
`b - 1` digits. The number of copies is at most
`T (e (T + 4) b / (b - T - 5) + 1) + e b / (b - 2) + 1`, using
`z_j <= T + 4` and `delta_j >= (b - T - 5)/(e b)`.

Step 3 (the string). Let `a_1, ..., a_T` be digits 1 to `T` of `F(L)`, and
`c = L + {a_1, ..., a_T} +` zeros, with `b - 1` digits in all. By (b) at
least one zero is left. Then `F(c) = F(L) + E` with
`E = sum w(a_i) + (number of zeros) m <= T x*^(x*) + b < P_(T+1)`. The part
of `F(L)` below digit `T` is less than `(b - 1) P_(T+1)` because digit
`T + 1` is at most `b - 2`, so adding `E` causes no carry into digit `T`.
So `F(c)` has `b - 1` digits and the top digits `a_1, ..., a_T`. Let `n` be
the string `a_1 ... a_T` followed by the other digits of `c` in any order.
It is an arrangement of `c`, so `F(n) = F(c)`, and `n` and `F(n)` share
their top `T` digits: `|n - F(n)| < P_T = b^(b-1-T)`. If `n = F(n)`,
exchange the digit `b - 1` of the first copy and a zero, both below the top
`T` positions. The new `n'` has `F(n') = F(n) = n`, differs from `n`, and
shares its top `T` digits, so `0 < |n' - F(n')| < P_T`. Finally `F(L)`
only grows, so `a_1 >= floor(b theta_1) >= b/e - 1`.

Corollary 35.14 (proved). Let `T(b)` be the largest `T` allowed by (a) and
(b). Then `T(b) = sqrt(b / e) - O(1)`, and `T(b)` does not decrease with
`b`: for fixed `T` the left sides of (a) and (b) fall and the right sides
grow with `b`. Every base `b` with `T(b) >= 1`, that is every `b >= 27`,
has near-solutions with `|n - F(n)| < n b^(-t)` for
`t = T(b) - log_b(e b / (b - e))`. This `t` does not decrease with `b`. It
is at least 1 for `b >= 49` and at least 2 for `b >= 77`.

Proof. Condition (b) reads `e T^2 (1 + O(T/b)) + O(T) <= b`, and condition
(a) holds for `T = O(sqrt b)`. The bound on `|n - F(n)|` is Theorem 35.13
with `b^(1-T)/(b/e - 1) = b^(-T) e b/(b - e)`. The thresholds 27, 49 and 77
are computed (`r6h_near.py bound`) and extend to all larger `b` by the
monotonicity.

Computed (`r6h_near.py bound`): `T(b) = 1` at 27, 3 at 100, 8 at 300, 16 at
1000, 30 at 3000, 58 at `10^4`, 189 at `10^5`, 603 at `10^6`. For `b` from
1000 to 200000, `T(b) - sqrt(b / e)` lies between -3.6 and -2.5.

Remarks.

- The answer to question 100 is yes for every fixed `t`, in every base
  `b >= b0(t)` with `b0(t)` about `e (t + 3.3)^2` (77 for `t = 2`, 493 for
  `t = 10`), and more: `t` can grow like
  `sqrt(b / e)`. The question's attack ("fix the counts of the largest
  digits ... by monotonicity") works. The digits of the real numbers
  `b^b sum m_y e^(-y) b^(-y)` never need to be known: it is enough that
  each steering step moves the steered digit by less than one unit, or by
  a jump that clears the zone.
- The limit `sqrt(b)` comes from the zone. The `T` matched digits must have
  weights below the `T`-th place, so a zone of about `T` values is
  forbidden for each, and steering out of it can cost `e T` copies.
- The model says much more (Heuristic). It puts many near-solutions with
  `|n - F(n)|` of size `b` in every base (section 11.1), that is `t` close
  to `b - 2`. Between `sqrt(b)` and `b` nothing is proved.
