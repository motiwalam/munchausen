# Round 6: one heuristic and existence

DRAFT. This file answers questions 91, 92, 108, 101 and 100 of
`questions-round6.md` (workload 4). Questions 91 and 92 ask whether the
root heuristic of short strings (sections 11.4, 19.3) and the per-base
model of long strings (chapter 5) are one heuristic. Question 108 asks for
one invariant-residue statement for every positional system. Question 101
asks whether averaging over bases pushes Theorem 20.3 past its barrier.
Question 100 asks for near-solutions that provably exist in every base.

Each statement carries one label: Theorem, Proposition, Lemma or Corollary
(proved here), Heuristic, Conjecture, or computed (an exact computation or
a measurement, with its range). Scripts are `analysis/r6h_*.py`, data
`analysis/data/r6h_*`. Every run used one or two threads.

Notation. `w(x) = x^x` for `x >= 1` and `w(0) = m`, the convention
`0^0 = m`. For a digit multiset `c`, `F(c)` is the sum of the weights,
`sigma(c)` the sum of the digits, `g(x) = w(x) - x` and
`G(c) = F(c) - sigma(c)`, the sum of `g` over `c`. The library writes
`S(c)` for `F(c)` in chapter 5 and question 91, and for `sigma(c)` in
chapter 20 and question 101; this file avoids the letter. An arrangement of
`c` is a string with the multiset `c` and a nonzero leading digit, and
`P_s(r)` is the value of the string `s` read in base `r`. `e(t)` is
`exp(2 pi i t)`. `X_k(b)` is the largest digit `x` with `x^x < b^k`.

## 1. Arrangements and the local factor (question 91)

### 1.1 The theorem

Fix a base `b`, a modulus `q` and a multiset `c` of `k >= 3` digits. Draw an
arrangement of `c` uniformly. Section 11.4 and direction A2 call
`q P(n == F(c) (mod q))` the local factor of `c` at `q`. For `q | b - 1`
every arrangement has `n == sigma(c)`, so the factor is `q` or 0, the
identity of section 1.6. For `q | b` the residue `n mod q` is the last digit
modulo `q`. The question is the case `gcd(q, b (b - 1)) = 1`. Since
`b (b - 1)` is even, such a `q` is odd: the moduli 4 and 8 of question 91
never qualify.

Condition on the leading digit `x0`. The other `k' = k - 1` digits form a
multiset `c'` and fill the positions `0, ..., k - 2` uniformly. Put

    gamma = max over h != 0 (mod q) of | (1/k') sum over x of c'_x e(h x / q) |.

`gamma = 1` exactly when all digits of `c'` are congruent modulo some prime
factor of `q`. For digits spread over the residues `gamma` is small.

Theorem 1.1 (proved). Let `gcd(q, b (b - 1)) = 1`, and let `m >= 0` with
`2m <= k'`. For every residue `a`,

    | q P(n == a (mod q) | leading digit x0) - 1 |
        <= (q - 1) prod over j < m of sqrt( (1 + rho_j^2) / 2 ),
    rho_j = min(1, (gamma k' + 2j) / (k' - 2j)).

Proof. Let `s` be a uniform arrangement of `c'` on the positions
`0..k'-1`. Pair the positions `(2j, 2j+1)` for `j < m`, and let
`tau_0, ..., tau_(m-1)` be independent fair coins, independent of `s`. Let
`s'` be `s` with the digits of pair `j` exchanged when `tau_j = 1`. The
arrangements of `c'` form one orbit of the permutations of the positions,
and composing a uniform element of an orbit with an independent
permutation keeps it uniform. So `s'` is uniform too. Exchanging the
digits `u = s_(2j)` and `v = s_(2j+1)` changes the value by

    delta_j = (v - u)(b^(2j) - b^(2j+1)) = (v - u) b^(2j) (1 - b),

a unit multiple of `v - u` modulo `q`, because `q` is prime to `b` and to
`b - 1`. Given `s`,

    E[ e(h n(s') / q) | s ] = e(h n(s) / q) prod over j < m of (1 + e(h delta_j / q)) / 2,

and `|(1 + e(t))/2| = |cos(pi t)|`. Now reveal the pairs in order. Given the
first `j` pairs, pair `j` is an ordered pair of two distinct items drawn
from the remaining `k'' = k' - 2j` digits, with counts `a_x`. For
`H = 2 h b^(2j) (1 - b)`, which is nonzero modulo `q` because `q` is odd,

    E[ cos(2 pi h delta_j / q) | past ] = (|A(H)|^2 - k'') / (k'' (k'' - 1)) <= |A(H)|^2 / k''^2,

with `A(H) = sum over x of a_x e(H x / q)`. The inequality is
`|A| <= k''`. Removing `2j` digits changes `A` by at most `2j`, so
`|A(H)| <= gamma k' + 2j`. By Cauchy-Schwarz the conditional mean of
`|cos(pi h delta_j / q)|` is at most `sqrt((1 + rho_j^2)/2)`, and
conditioning pair by pair bounds the mean of the product by the product of
these bounds. Finally `P(n == a) = (1/q) sum over h of e(-h a/q) E e(h n/q)`,
and the term `h = 0` is `1/q`.

Corollary 1.2 (proved). With `2m <= (1 - gamma) k' / 4`,

    | q P(n == a (mod q)) - 1 | <= (q - 1) e^(1/8) exp( -(1 - gamma)^2 (k - 1) / 64 ),

for every residue `a`, also without conditioning on the leading digit (if
the bound holds for each `x0`, it holds for the mixture, with `gamma` the
largest over the `x0`).

Proof. Take `eps = (1 - gamma)/4` and `m = floor(eps k' / 2)`. Then
`rho_j <= (gamma + eps)/(1 - eps) = (3 gamma + 1)/(3 + gamma) <= 1 - (1 - gamma)/2`,
so `(1 + rho_j^2)/2 <= 1 - (1 - gamma)/4` and each factor is at most
`exp(-(1 - gamma)/8)`. Then `m >= (1 - gamma) k'/8 - 1`.

Check (computed, 200 random multisets with `k = 4..12`, bases 20 to 30,
`q` from 3 to 13 prime to `b (b - 1)`, exact shares of `r6h_arrange.py`):
the deviation `max over a of |q P(n == a) - 1|` never exceeds 0.63 times
the bound of Theorem 1.1 with the best `m`.

Remarks.

- The rate does not involve `ord_q(b)`. Question 91 expected an error
  exponentially small in `k / ord_q(b)`, from the class sums of positions
  with equal `b^i mod q`. The exchange of two adjacent digits avoids the
  classes: it always moves `n` by a unit times the digit difference. The
  only place where `b` enters is the requirement that `b` and `b - 1` be
  units modulo `q`.
- For a long string (`k` near `b`) the digits are close to uniform on
  `0..b-1`. Since `|sum over x < b of e(h x/q)| <= q/2`, `gamma` is at most
  `q/(2b)` plus a fluctuation of order `k^(-1/2)`. So for `q <= b/10` prime
  to `b (b - 1)`, `gamma <= 0.1` once `k` is large, and every residue has
  `q P(n == a) = 1 + O(q e^(-k/80))`. This is the sense in which
  "arrangements make the local factors vanish for long strings".
- The constants are not optimized. When the digits are small compared with
  `q`, `gamma` is close to 1 although the arrangements still spread: the
  units `b^(2j)(1 - b)` vary with `j`, which the bound ignores.

### 1.2 The root factor is the base average of the per-base factor

For a string `s` let `N_s(q)` be the number of `r (mod q)` with
`P_s(r) == F(s) (mod q)`. For a prime `q` this is the factor `rho_s(q)` of
section 11.4, and for a prime power the p-adic factor of section 19.3. For a
residue `r` let `share_r(a)` be the share of the arrangements of `c` with
`P_s(r) == a (mod q)`; it depends only on `r mod q`.

Proposition 1.3 (proved). For every multiset `c` and every `q >= 2`,

    mean over the arrangements s of c of N_s(q) = sum over r mod q of share_r(F(c) mod q).

The term `r == 1` is `1[q | G(c)]`. For a prime `q` the term `r == 0` is the
share of the arrangements whose last digit is `== F(c) (mod q)`, and the
other `q - 2` terms are `1/q` each up to the error of Theorem 1.1.

Proof. Exchange the sum over `r` and the mean over the arrangements. For
`r == 1`, `P_s(1) = sigma(c)` for every arrangement. For `r == 0`,
`P_s(0)` is the last digit. The rest is Theorem 1.1.

So the root heuristic, at a fixed string, averages over the residue of the
base. The per-base refined model fixes the residue: at bases
`b == 1 (mod q)` its factor is `q 1[q | G(c)]`, at other bases it is the
arrangement factor of Theorem 1.1 or the last-digit factor. Averaged over
`b mod q`, the per-base factors give `(1/q)(q 1[q|G] + q share_0 + ...)`,
which is the mean of `N_s(q)`. For a random multiset the mean is
`1 + (P(q | G(c)) - 1/q)`: the root heuristic's excess at the prime `q` is
the excess of the identity modulo `q` at the bases where `q | b - 1`.

Computed (`r6h_arrange.py rho`, `data/r6h_arrange_rho.txt`, 30 random
candidate multisets per cell, bases 9 to 30). The identity holds to
`4 * 10^-16` against a brute-force mean over all arrangements for
`k <= 6`. The part of the residues `r` prime to `q` other than `r = 1` is
0.32 to 0.41 for `q = 3` (1/3 expected), 0.59 to 0.69 for `q = 5` (3/5),
0.60 to 0.75 for `q = 7` (5/7) at `k = 3..6`, and within 0.01 of these
values for `k = 7..12`.

### 1.4 Where the factor 2 to 4 comes from

Direction A2 and question 91 explain the excess of short solutions over the
per-base model by arrangement factors that stay away from 1 at short
lengths. Proposition 1.3 and Table 1.3.1 say otherwise: those factors have
mean 1, so they add scatter, not an excess. The excess has a different
source. At a short length the factor of the identity modulo `b - 1`,
`(b - 1) P(b - 1 | G(c))`, is far above its long-string limit, because a
sum of few values `g(x) = x^x - x` of small digits is concentrated modulo
the small primes of `b - 1` (for example `x^x == x (mod 3)` for 5 of every
6 digits `x >= 1`). The basic model leaves this out, and the refined model
of section 5.3 contains it exactly if it is computed at the short length
itself.

Heuristic 1.4. The root heuristic and the per-base refined model are two
averages of one local density. The root heuristic averages the per-base
factors over the residue of the base modulo each `q`; the per-base model
keeps the residue of the actual base. They agree on totals over complete
residue systems of bases. Per base they differ by the arrangement factors
at `q` prime to `b (b - 1)`, which have mean 1 and an RMS of about
`e^(-0.35 k)`, and by the last-digit factor at `q | b`. The excess of short
solutions over the basic per-base model is the identity factor modulo
`b - 1` at the short length (a factor 1.1 to 2.1 for `x^x`, 3 for the
narcissistic weights of section 19.4, where the refined model already
matches), plus identity families. It is not an arrangement effect. The
crossover where the arrangement factors become negligible is about 10
digits; the identity factor converges more slowly in the small primes of
`b - 1`; under `0^0 = 1` its long-string limit, averaged over the
parities, is about 1.07 (1 in even bases, 1.135 in odd ones), and Table
1.4.1 has 1.07 to 1.11 from 8 digits on, against 1.4 to 1.5 at 3 and 4
digits.

## 2. The two heuristics at medium lengths (question 92)

### 2.1 The computation

`r6h_medium.py` computes, for every base `b` from 7 to 20, every length `k`
from 6 to 12 with `k <= b` (83 cells per convention) and both conventions:

- root: the root heuristic of sections 11.4 and 19.3 for the strings with
  digits below `b` whose real root lies in `[b - 1/2, b + 1/2)`, by Monte
  Carlo (1.5 million strings per cell under `0^0 = 1`, 0.6 million under
  `0^0 = 0`; digits up to `min(b - 1, X)` with `X^X < (b + 1/2)^k`). Three
  versions: spacing only, local factors modulo the primes up to 31
  ("primes"), and roots modulo 64, 81, 125 in place of 2, 3, 5 ("p-adic").
- ref: the refined per-base model, exact (`r6q_lib.refined_by_zeros`), and
  the same times the forced-top factor for the top `t = 1, 2, 3` digits
  (the factor of `forced_top.py`, sampled over the candidate multisets that
  meet the identity, one million draws per cell).
- win: the window calibration of section 22.4.1 (`r4b_spectrum.c`, class
  mean modulo `2(b - 1)`, window `b <= |D| <= 0.05 b^(k-1)`), lengths 6 to 10
  only; length 11 would take about 40 minutes and length 12 several hours.
- obs: the census of `output/b2-41-<m>.txt`.

### 2.3 What the numbers say

- The two heuristics predict the same total at lengths 6 to 12, within 10%
  under `0^0 = 1` (8.85 against 9.51 and 9.74) and within 12% under
  `0^0 = 0` (12.72 against 13.00 and 14.19). The census, 10 and 12, fits
  both (computed against heuristic). So direction A2 holds at medium
  lengths in the sense of Heuristic 1.4: on totals over many bases the
  root heuristic with local factors and the refined per-base model are the
  same prediction.
- They split the total differently between bases, as Proposition 1.3
  predicts. The refined model gives the parity doubling to the odd bases
  only; the root heuristic averages its 2-adic factor over both parities.
  Under `0^0 = 0`, lengths 6 to 10, the window calibration has 2.38 in even
  bases and 5.77 in odd ones (ratio 2.4); the refined model with `t = 1`
  has 2.55 and 5.56; the root heuristic with primes has 4.37 and 5.27, and
  the p-adic version 4.33 and 6.79. The census has 2 and 8. So per base the
  refined model matches the window calibration and the root heuristic does
  not.
- The forced top digits matter at these lengths in single cells. In base 10
  at 6 digits the refined model gives 0.12, the forced-top model 0.004 to
  0.000, the window calibration 0.002, and the root heuristic 0.05 to 0.15.
  The candidate digits are at most 7, and a single 7 forces the top digit 8,
  which no candidate can contain. The root heuristic smooths over the base
  and cannot see this. On totals the forced-top factors change little
  (0 to 11%).
- So the answer to question 92 is: the refined model with the top digit
  forced matches the window calibration best, cell by cell and by parity;
  the root heuristic matches it on totals only. No missing factor shows at
  these lengths. The 5-digit excess of section 22.4.3 is therefore not a
  factor that the medium lengths share (Heuristic).

## 3. One invariant-residue proposition (question 108)

### 3.1 The proposition

A positional system here is: a commutative ring `R` (the integers,
`Z[i]`, `Z[omega]`, `Z[eps]`, `Z[1/q]` or `F_q[T]`), place values
`beta_i` in `R` for the positions `i` of an index set, a digit set `D` in
`R`, and a set `A` of admissible strings. A string `s` has a finite set of
positions, a digit `s_i` at each, the value `v(s) = sum s_i beta_i`, and the
multiset of its digits. Two admissible strings are equivalent when they
have the same multiset. An elementary exchange is a pair of admissible
strings on the same positions that differ by swapping the digits at two
positions `i, j`. For an ideal `I` of `R`, say that the multiset fixes `v`
modulo `I` when equivalent admissible strings have congruent values.

Proposition 3.1 (proved). Let `I_0` be the ideal generated by all
`v(s) - v(s')` with `s, s'` equivalent and admissible, and `J` the ideal
generated by `(s_i - s_j)(beta_j - beta_i)` over the elementary exchanges.

1. The multiset fixes `v` modulo `I` exactly when `I` contains `I_0`.
2. `J` lies in `I_0`, and `J = I_0` when the admissible strings of each
   multiset are connected by elementary exchanges.
3. Let `beta_i = u beta^i` with `u` a unit, `i` in an interval of integers
   (negative `i` only when `beta` is a unit). Then every value satisfies
   `v(s) == u sum s_i (mod beta - 1)`, so `I_0` lies in `(beta - 1)`. If some
   admissible string allows the exchange of two adjacent positions `i`,
   `i + 1` whose digits differ by a unit, and `beta^i` is a unit (always for
   `i = 0`), then `I_0 = (beta - 1)`.

Proof. (1) and the first half of (2) are the definitions; an elementary
exchange changes the value by `s_i beta_j + s_j beta_i - s_i beta_i - s_j beta_j`.
If the exchange graph of each class is connected, every difference
`v(s) - v(s')` is a sum of elementary differences along a path. (3)
`beta^i - beta^j = beta^j (beta^(i-j) - 1)` lies in `(beta - 1)`, so
`beta_i == u (mod beta - 1)`. An exchange at positions `i, i + 1` with
digit difference a unit `e` puts `e u beta^i (beta - 1)` into `J`, a unit
multiple of `beta - 1`.

In the integer, negative, bijective and balanced bases the exchange of
the last two digits of a string with at least 3 digits is admissible, so
(3) gives `I_0` exactly. When the admissible strings are the expansions of
rational integers in a system whose place values are not integers (`p/q`
and `-p/q` with `q > 1`, the Gaussian, Eisenstein and Pisot bases), an
exchange usually leaves `Z`. Then (2) gives no lower bound for `I_0`, and
Table 3.2.1 checks `I_0 meet Z` directly.

Question 108 states (1) with "exactly when `I` contains every difference
`beta_i - beta_j` of two positions that can exchange". That needs the
digit differences and the connectivity of (2). Without a unit digit
difference the ideal can be smaller (digits all even give `2 (beta - 1)`),
and without connectivity it can be larger than `J`.

Proposition 3.2 (proved). Let `beta` be an algebraic integer with monic
minimal polynomial `f` over `Z`, and `R = Z[beta]`. Then
`(beta - 1) R` meets `Z` in `|f(1)| Z`. For `R = Z[1/q]` and
`beta = p/q` or `-p/q` with `gcd(p, q) = 1`, `(beta - 1)` meets `Z` in
`(p - q) Z` or `(p + q) Z`.

Proof. `Z[t]/(f(t), t - 1) = Z/f(1)`. In `Z[1/q]` the element
`p/q - 1 = (p - q)/q` is a unit times `p - q`, and `Z[1/q]/(p - q) = Z/(p - q)`
since `gcd(q, p - q) = 1`; the same for `-p/q - 1 = -(p + q)/q`.

So the integer modulus of a geometric system is `|f(1)|`: `b - 1` for
`beta = b`, `b + 1` for `-b`, `(n+1)^2 + 1` for `-n + i`, `n^2 + 3n + 3` for
`-n + omega`, `x` for `eps_x` (`f = t^2 - x t - 1`), `B - 1` in `F_q[T]`,
`p - q` for `p/q` (with the place values `(1/q)(p/q)^i` of section 21.2,
`u = 1/q`), and `p + q` for `-p/q`. The identity modulus, the swap-lemma
class of offsets (Lemmas 26.1 and 26.4: equivalent strings have offsets
`D` congruent modulo `I_0`, and the elementary exchanges realize the
generators) and the residue condition of the refined model are all this
ideal.

Proposition 3.3 (proved, the residue factor). Let the digits of a string be
independent with a fixed law that gives positive weight to a digit `x1`
with `g(x1) = 0` (the digit 1). Let `H` be the subgroup of `R/I_0`
generated by the `g(x)` of the digits with positive weight, and suppose
`R/I_0` is finite. Then `P(sum g(x_i) == 0 (mod I_0)) -> 1/|H|` as the
length grows, so the refined model gives the factor `[R/I_0 : H]`.

Proof. The partial sums are a random walk on the finite group `H` whose
step law charges 0 and generates `H`; it is irreducible and aperiodic, so
it converges to the uniform law on `H`.

This is the parity doubling (index 2 when all `g(x)` are even), the
factor `q^k(B)` of `F_q[T]` (Theorem 21.6: the trace conditions cut out
`H`), and the absence of any factor in the factorial system (`I_0 = R`).
Proposition 30.6 is the case of the weights `x^d`. For `x^x` in base `b`
the law of the digits changes with `b`, so Proposition 3.3 is the model
statement, and Theorem 30.5 is the theorem.

## 4. Beyond `ln b / ln ln b` by averaging over bases (question 101)

### 4.1 The averaged count

Notation of section 20.1: `L = ln B`, `K = kappa L / ln L`, `X = X_K(B)`,
`m(c) = F(c) - sigma(c) = G(c)`. For a base `b` choose a threshold `z(b)`
such that `k (z+1)^(z+1)` is below `b - 1`; call the digits up to `z`
small. For a multiset `c` write `c = c_L + c_S` (large and small parts).

Proposition 4.1 (proved). For `k >= 3` and `b` large, the base of a
`k`-digit solution with multiset `c` and leading digit `p` lies in the
window of Lemma 20.1 computed from `F(c_L)`, widened by one base. Given
`c_L` and `b`, the congruence `b - 1 | m(c)` fixes `m(c_S)` (it lies in
`[0, b - 2]`), so the number of bases `b` in `(sqrt B, B]` with a `k`-digit
solution is at most

    sum over c_L, p, and b in the widened window of r(b, c_L),
    r(b, c_L) = #{ c_S : m(c_S) == -m(c_L) (mod b - 1) } <= max over N of r_j(N),

with `r_j(N)` the number of small multisets of `j` digits with
`m(c_S) = N`. By Lemma 30.2 the digits of `c_S` from
`z0 = max(2, ceil((j - 1)/2))` up are fixed by `m(c_S)` once the digits
below `z0` are fixed, so `r_j(N) <= C(z0 + j, j)`.

Proof. `F(c) - F(c_L) = F(c_S) < b`, which moves `(F/p)^(1/(k-1))` by less
than 1 for `k >= 3`. The rest is the statement of question 101 with
Lemma 30.2.

### 4.2 Why the gain is bounded

Proposition 4.2 (proved). Let `A > 0`, `z = z_A(B)` the largest `z` with
`z^z <= B^A`, and `K = kappa L / ln L` with `kappa` bounded. Among the
multisets of `K` digits from `0..X`, the share with no digit `<= z` is at
least `exp(-K (z + 1)/(X - z))`, and `K (z + 1)/(X - z) -> 2A`. So the
share is at least `e^(-2A - o(1))`.

Proof. The share is `C(X - z - 1 + K, K) / C(X + K, K)`, the product over
`i = 1..K` of `(X - z - 1 + i)/(X + i)`, which is at least
`(1 - (z+1)/(X+1))^K`, and `1 - t >= exp(-t/(1 - t))`. From
`X ln X = K L (1 + o(1))` we get `K / X = (ln X / L)(1 + o(1))` and
`ln X = (2 + o(1)) ln L`. From `z ln z = A L (1 + o(1))` we get
`z = A L / ln z (1 + o(1))` and `ln z = (1 + o(1)) ln L`. Hence
`K z / X = A ln X / ln z (1 + o(1)) -> 2A`.

So every multiset that matters to Theorem 20.3 has `2A` digits of weight
below `B^A` on average, and a share `e^(-2A)` has none. For those, the
averaged count of Proposition 4.1 is the count of Theorem 20.3 itself:
`r = 1[b - 1 | m(c_L)]`, bounded by 1. Small weights (below `b`) need
`A <= 1`, so the route of question 101 gains at most a factor `e^(2 + o(1))`
over Theorem 20.3, and it moves the threshold of Theorem 20.3 (d) by
`O(1/ln L)` digits. The first look reported in the task (a gain of
`B^(o(1))`) is right, and in fact the gain is bounded.

Computed (`r6h_average.py`, `data/r6h_average.txt`). The bound of
Proposition 20.2 (it reproduces `B^0.973` at `K = 48` and the threshold
`K = 49` of section 20.1.3) against the averaged bound of Proposition 4.1
with `z` the largest digit with `60 z^z < sqrt(B)` (`z = 30` at `10^96`) and
`r_j` bounded as above.

What would be needed. The terms that dominate are large multisets and the
indicator `1[b - 1 | m(c_L)]` over the few bases of their windows. A gain
in the exponent needs, on average over the windows, that sums of weights
`x^x` with `z < x <= X` avoid the residue 0 modulo `b - 1` at the rate
`1/b`: equidistribution of these sums in short windows of moduli, as
section 20.1.5 says. Anti-concentration in the style of chapter 30 cannot
supply it, since it lives on the small digits, which typical multisets
lack.

Heuristic 4.5. If `m(c_L)` behaved like a random integer modulo the window
bases, the averaged count would lose a factor `B^(1 - o(1))`, and the range
would reach `K` with `C(X + K, K)` about `B^2`, that is `alpha` near 2, as
section 20.1.5 says.

### 4.3 What averaging over the window does give

The window does not see the small digits at all, and that gives a
statement beyond `ln b / ln ln b` for a class of strings.

Theorem 4.4 (proved). Fix `C >= 1` and `eps > 0`. Call a digit `x` of a
solution in base `b` large when `x^x >= sqrt(b)`. The number of bases
`b <= B` with a solution of length at most `(ln B)^C` that has at most
`(1/C - eps) ln B / ln ln B` large digits is at most
`B^(max(1/2, 1 - C eps) + o(1))`.
So almost all bases `b` have no solution of length at most `(ln b)^C`
with at most `(1/C - eps) ln b / ln ln b` digits of weight at least
`sqrt(b)`, whatever the number of smaller digits.

Proof. Bases up to `sqrt(B)` are at most `sqrt(B)`. Take `b > sqrt(B)` and
a solution `n` of `k <= K2 = L^C` digits, multiset `c = c_L + c_S` with
`c_L` the large digits, `k' = |c_L| <= K' = (1/C - eps) L / ln L`, and
leading digit `p`. Every digit is at most `X = X_(K2)(B)`, and
`X ln X < K2 L`, so `ln X <= (C + 1) ln L`. Each digit string is a solution in at
most one base (Proposition 1.3), so at most `(X + 1)^2` bases have a
2-digit solution; take `k >= 3`. Then `F(c_L) >= F(c) - k sqrt(b) >= p b^(k-1) / 2`.
Since `F(c_S) <= k sqrt(b)`, the mean value theorem for
`t -> (t/p)^(1/(k-1))` moves `Y = (F(c)/p)^(1/(k-1))` by at most
`k sqrt(b) / ((k - 1) p (b/2)^(k-2)) < 1` from `Y' = (F(c_L)/p)^(1/(k-1))`
for large `B`. By Lemma 20.1, `b` lies in an interval of length
`(M + 1)/(p (k - 1)) + 2` determined by `(c_L, k, p)`. Map the base to
`(c_L, k, p, b)`; the map is injective. The number of images is at most

    sum over k' <= K', k <= K2, p <= X of C(X + k', k') ((X + 1)/(p (k - 1)) + 3)
        <= (K' + 1) K2 (X + 1) (H_X + 3) C(X + K', K').

Now `ln C(X + K', K') <= K' ln(e (X + K')/K') = K' (C ln L + O(ln ln L))
= (1 - C eps) L (1 + o(1))`, and the other factors are `exp(O(ln L))`. The
statement for almost all `b` follows as in Theorem 20.3 (c), since the
conditions are monotone in `b`.

For `C = 1` this covers lengths up to `ln b`, beyond the
`ln b / ln ln b` of Theorem 20.3, for strings with at most
`(1 - eps) ln b / ln ln b` large digits. Typical strings of such lengths
have almost all digits large, so Theorem 4.4 says nothing about them. It
covers the structured strings: a few large digits padded with zeros, ones
and other small digits, which is the shape of the self-paying families of
chapter 2 and the binomial families of chapter 22. Those families live in
a set of bases of density 0 (perfect powers and the like), as Theorem 4.4
requires.

## 5. Near-solutions in every base (question 100)

### 5.1 The theorem

Write `d = b - 1` for the length, `P_j = b^(b-1-j)` for the place value of
the `j`-th digit from the top (digit 1 at position `b - 2`), and
`theta_i = (1 - i/b)^(b-i)`, so that `(b - i)^(b-i) = theta_i b^(b-i)`.
From `-x/(1-x) <= ln(1 - x) <= -x`, `e^(-i) <= theta_i <= e^(-i + i^2/b)`.

Theorem 5.1 (proved). Let `b >= 3` and `T >= 1` with

- (a) `T + 5 <= b (1 - e^(-1 + 1/b))` and `2 (T + 4) <= b`;
- (b) `1 + T (e (T + 4) b / (b - T - 5) + 1) + e b / (b - 2) + 1 + T <= b - 2`.

Then for each convention there is an `n` with `b - 1` digits and

    0 < | n - F(n) | < b^(b - 1 - T),

and `n >= (b/e - 1) b^(b-2)`, so `|n - F(n)| < n b^(1-T) / (b/e - 1)`.

Proof. Let `x*` be the largest `x` with `T x^x + b < P_(T+1)`. Since
`x^x < b^x`, `x* >= b - T - 4`. The zones are `Z_j = [x*, b - 1]` for
`1 <= j <= T` and `Z_(T+1) = {b - 1}`; `z_j = |Z_j| <= T + 4`.

Step 1 (start). `L` holds one copy of `b - 1`. Then
`F(L) = theta_1 b^(b-1)` has `b - 1` digits, and its digit 1,
`floor(b theta_1) <= b e^(-1 + 1/b)`, is at most `x* - 1` by (a).

Step 2 (steering). For `j = 2, ..., T + 1` in turn: if digit `j` of
`F(L)` lies in `Z_j`, choose the steering digit `x_j`, the largest `x` with
`x^x <= (b - z_j - 1) P_j`, and add copies of it to `L` until digit `j`
leaves `Z_j`. Let `s = x_j^(x_j) / P_j`. Since
`(x+1)^(x+1) <= e (x+1) x^x`, either `x_j = b - 1` and `s >= b theta_1 >= b/e`,
or `s > (b - z_j - 1)/(e b) =: delta_j`. Also `s <= b - z_j - 1`. Let
`N_j = floor(F / P_j)`, the number formed by digits 1 to `j`. One copy
raises `N_j` by `floor(s)` or `floor(s) + 1`.

- If `s >= z_j`, one copy moves digit `j` from `r >= b - z_j` to
  `r + J - b` with `z_j <= J <= b - z_j`, so out of `Z_j`.
- If `1 <= s < z_j`, each copy moves `N_j` by 1 to `z_j`, and after at most
  `z_j` copies digit `j` has wrapped past `b - 1` to a value at most
  `z_j - 1 < b - z_j`.
- If `s < 1`, each copy raises `N_j` by 0 or 1, so `N_j` passes through
  every integer, and at most `z_j / delta_j + 1` copies bring digit `j` to 0.

In every case we stop at the first copy after which digit `j` is out of
`Z_j`, so `N_(j-1)` grows by exactly 1 if digit `j` wrapped and by 0
otherwise. Digit `j - 1` was at most `x* - 1` after its own step, so it
becomes at most `x*` and causes no further carry. Later steps change `N_j`
by at most 1 for the same reason. So after step 2, digits 1 to `T` are at
most `x*`, digit `T + 1` is at most `b - 2`, and no carry ever reached
beyond digit 1, so `F(L)` keeps `b - 1` digits. The number of copies is at
most `T (e (T + 4) b / (b - T - 5) + 1) + e b / (b - 2) + 1`, using
`z_j <= T + 4` and `delta_j >= (b - T - 5)/(e b)`.

Step 3 (the string). Let `a_1, ..., a_T` be digits 1 to `T` of `F(L)`, and
`c = L + {a_1, ..., a_T} + ` zeros, with `b - 1` digits in all; by (b) at
least one zero is left. Then `F(c) = F(L) + E` with
`E = sum w(a_i) + (number of zeros) m <= T x*^(x*) + b < P_(T+1)`. The part
of `F(L)` below digit `T` is less than `(b - 1) P_(T+1)` because digit
`T + 1` is at most `b - 2`, so adding `E` causes no carry into digit `T`:
`F(c)` has `b - 1` digits and the top digits `a_1, ..., a_T`. Let `n` be the
string `a_1 ... a_T` followed by the other digits of `c` in any order. It
is an arrangement of `c`, so `F(n) = F(c)`, and `n` and `F(n)` share their
top `T` digits: `|n - F(n)| < P_T = b^(b-1-T)`. If `n = F(n)`, exchange
the digit `b - 1` and a zero below the top `T` positions; the new `n'` has
`F(n') = F(n) = n != n'`. Finally `a_1 >= floor(b theta_1) >= b/e - 1`.

Corollary 5.2 (proved). Let `T(b)` be the largest `T` allowed by (a) and
(b). Then `T(b) = sqrt(b / e) - O(1)`, and every base `b` with
`T(b) >= 1`, that is every `b >= 27` (for `T = 1` the left sides of (a)
and (b) fall and the right sides grow with `b`), has near-solutions with
`|n - F(n)| < n b^(-t)` for `t = T(b) - log_b(e b / (b - e)) `. Computed
(`r6h_near.py bound`): `T(b) = 1` at 27, 3 at 100, 8 at 300, 16 at 1000,
30 at 3000, 58 at `10^4`, 189 at `10^5`, 603 at `10^6`. For `b` from 1000 to 200000,
`T(b) - sqrt(b / e)` lies between -3.6 and -2.5.

Proof. Condition (b) reads `e T^2 (1 + O(T/b)) + O(T) <= b`. Condition (a)
holds for `T = O(sqrt b)`.

Remarks.

- The answer to question 100 is yes for every `t` and more: `t` can grow
  like `sqrt(b / e)`. The question's attack ("fix the counts of the largest
  digits ... by monotonicity") works, and the digits of the real numbers
  `b^b sum m_y e^(-y) b^(-y)` never need to be known: it is enough that
  each steering step moves the steered digit by less than one unit, or by
  a jump that clears the zone.
- The limit `sqrt(b)` comes from the zone: the `T` matched digits must have
  weights below the `T`-th place, so a zone of about `T` values is
  forbidden for each, and steering out of it can cost `e T` copies.
- The model says much more. It puts many near-solutions with
  `|n - F(n)|` of size `b` in every base (section 11.1), that is `t` close
  to `b - 2`. Between `sqrt(b)` and `b` nothing is proved.

Every base from 6 to 200 reaches `T >= 1`; from 10 to 200 it reaches
`T >= 0.17 b`, and the sampled bases 300 to 1500 reach 0.13 `b` to 0.25 `b`. The reached `T` grows faster than `sqrt(b)` because most
digits never fall into their zone. A rough count (Heuristic): a digit falls
into its zone with chance about `T / b` and then costs up to `e T` copies, so
the budget `b` allows `T` of order `b^(2/3)`; the data (0.13 `b` to 0.25 `b`
in bases 300 to 1500) are consistent with this but do not test it.

## 6. Library errors and problems

1. Notation. `S(c)` means the weight sum `F(c)` in chapter 5 (section 5.1)
   and in question 91 and direction A2 ("a value `== S(c) (mod q)`"), and
   the digit sum in chapter 20 and question 101 (`F(c) - S(c)`). The two
   meanings meet in round 6. One letter per meaning would help.
2. `questions-round6.md`, question 91: the moduli 4 and 8 can never be
   prime to `b (b - 1)`, which is always even. The rate "exponentially small
   in `k / ord_q(b)`" is not the right form: the proven rate depends on `k`
   and the spread of the digits, not on `ord_q(b)` (Theorem 1.1).
3. `questions-round6.md`, direction A2 and question 91: "a short string has
   few arrangements, and the factor is far from 1. That would explain why
   short solutions run 2 to 4 times above the per-base model". The
   arrangement factors at `q` prime to `b (b - 1)` have mean 1 at every
   length (Table 1.3.1); they cannot produce an excess. The excess over the
   basic model is the identity factor modulo `b - 1` at the short length
   (Table 1.4.1) plus identity families. Section 19.4 already shows this for
   the narcissistic weights, where the refined model matches and the basic
   model is low by 3.
4. `questions-round6.md`, question 108: "determines `n` modulo an ideal `I`
   exactly when `I` contains every difference `beta_i - beta_j` of two
   positions that can exchange their digits" omits the digit differences
   and the connectivity of the exchange graph (Proposition 3.1 (2) and the
   remark after it).
5. `questions-round6.md`, question 100, Why: "Each further matched digit
   adds 1 to `t`" and "that is where the method may stop": the method does
   not need the base-`b` digits of `b^b sum m_y e^(-y) b^(-y)`; Theorem 5.1
   matches about `sqrt(b / e)` digits.
6. Section 14.6.2 says the short factorions are four times too common "as
   are the short Munchausen numbers of section 10.5". Section 10.5
   compares with the basic model (`length_window.short_e`). Against the
   exact refined model the Munchausen short exceptions are 5 against 1.35
   under `0^0 = 1`, one of them a family member (Table 1.4.1 and the text
   after it), so the factor is about 3 on generic strings, not 4 to 5, and
   part of it is the known 5-digit excess.

## New questions

1. Linear `t`. Can near-solutions with `t >= c b` be proved in every base?
   The obstacle in Theorem 5.1 is the zone of the matched digits. A
   fixed-point version that lets matched digits be large and feeds their
   weights back into the steering might remove it.
2. The identity factor at short lengths, in closed form. Table 1.4.1
   computes `(b - 1) P(b - 1 | G(c))` by the exact walk. Is there a formula
   in the small primes of `b - 1` and the length, as in Proposition 3.3 but
   for the digits up to `X_k(b)`, that gives the short-solution rates of
   all worlds (Munchausen, factorions, narcissistic) at once?
3. Equidistribution on average over windows. Prove, for some
   `theta > 0`, that sums of `K` weights `x^x` with `z < x <= X` hit
   `0 (mod b - 1)` for at most a share `B^(-theta)` of the pairs
   (multiset, window base). By Proposition 4.1 this would extend Theorem
   20.3 to `alpha = 1 + theta`.
4. Sharper arrangement bounds. Replace `gamma` in Theorem 1.1 by a
   quantity that uses the varying units `b^(2j)(1 - b)`, so that strings
   with small digits and large `q` are covered, and compare with the
   measured `e^(-0.35 k)`.
5. Theorem 4.4 with the congruence. Combine the window count of Theorem 4.4
   with the condition `b - 1 | m(c)` for solutions whose small part is
   long and dissociated: does it give a range beyond `(ln b)^C` for strings
   with few large digits?
